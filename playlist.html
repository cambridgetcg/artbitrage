One track for each room in the house, chosen by ear and argued for in plain words. Artbitrage holds none of this music and grants no rights in any of it; what is here are facts about records, the way a library catalogue holds facts about books.

The first track is the whole thesis

Lost Frequencies' Are You With Me is not an original. It is a rework of a country song — Easton Corbin, 2012, written by Terry McBride, Shane McAnally and Josh Osborne — moved onto a dance floor and released in 2014, where it went to number one across Europe. Same song. Different room. The gap between the two rooms is where all the value appeared.

That is this whole project's founding line, arriving by accident in a track somebody put on because it sounded good. Art as arbitrage, literally. So it gets the front door.

Everything below was checked against MusicBrainz — an open catalogue of facts about music — in small batches, one search per track, because MusicBrainz is free and donation-funded and asks for no more than a request a second. Twenty-three of twenty-four survived. Three artist credits were wrong and are corrected in the open. One track did not exist at all.

A different kind of thing: not a recommendation, a permission. Every licence here was read on the page that states it, and the exact licence is named — CC0, CC BY 4.0 and CC BY-NC 4.0 grant wildly different things, and "Creative Commons" on its own grants nothing knowable.

Ten silent players, and a promise each

The promise ledger

Ten rooms in this house already carry an audio player. Every one of them holds the same forty-four-byte silent file, under a note saying what would be there in production. Nobody ever came back. Here is which of those promises can legally be kept — because the rule is that a composition goes free seventy years after its composer dies, while every recording of it carries its own separate copyright. Bach is free; a particular Bach recording is not.
