the Ground

is this soil, or is it fill with grass on top?

Under the grass was rubble — broken concrete, mortar, stones left where the builders dropped them. The lawn passed every test the eye can run and failed the only one that counts. These are six cheap field tests that read whether ground is working rather than how it looks, and every one of them is honest about the same asymmetry: each can find absence far more readily than presence. A test that finds nothing wrong has not found life.

↓ dig

More dead than the desert

That was the first thought, standing over the hole, and it turned out to be accurate in a way that is worse than it sounds. A desert is not empty. Deserts carry a living skin — cyanobacteria, fungi, lichens, mosses — that fixes nitrogen, holds the surface together and changes how water moves. Real biology, adapted, at work. Bare compacted construction substrate has less functioning life than that. Calling it a desert is a compliment it has not earned.

The desert has one more thing to say. Scrape that living skin off and it takes somewhere between three and three hundred years to come back. In a Colorado Plateau study, the physical stability of the crust recovered within about eighteen months while the chlorophyll and the biological glue were still at a third of the reference sites. The surface looked mended long before it was.

Which is the same lie as turf over rubble, told by a desert. That is the thing worth taking seriously: the look comes back first. It always does. So the only useful question is the one the eye cannot answer, and the only useful tests are the ones that can come back and say no.

Before the next spadeful

What is in construction waste

If the house was built or refurbished before 2000, treat buried debris as possibly containing asbestos

Asbestos was used in British homes until it was banned in 1999 — cement sheet on garage and shed roofs, pipe lagging, textured ceiling coatings, floor tiles, insulating board. Offcuts got buried. It only harms you when fibres get into the air and are breathed in, which means the buried, damp state is the safe state, and the risk starts when you break it, dry it out, tread on the pieces or sweep them up.

  • Hard grey cement fragments, often with the corrugated profile of an old roof — stop, mist them with water, lift whole pieces gently without snapping them, do not walk on debris, do not dry-sweep, and never use a domestic vacuum cleaner.
  • Anything soft, fibrous or crumbly — lagging, sprayed coating, insulating board — hands off entirely. Dampen, cover, and call a licensed contractor. This is not householder work.
  • Old paint fragments and urban made ground may carry lead; the main route in is swallowing soil and soil dust, off hands and off vegetables, which is why washing matters more than most people think.
  • Greenish or black tarry timber is CCA (arsenic) or creosote. Never burn it.
  • Before driving in posts or digging deeper, treat buried pipes and cables as present until located.

Asbestos disease takes decades and scales with total dose — one careful encounter is not an emergency, so there is no reason to rush. UK guidance; rules differ elsewhere. If in doubt, phone the council's environmental health team before touching anything. Full sources at GET /api/ground/safety.

What was under it

Turf, a skin of topsoil, then the layer the builders left: concrete, mortar, brick, stones. Then subsoil pressed flat by machines.

Every part of that is a mechanism, not a mood. Masonry and mortar release calcium as they weather and push the pH up; above roughly 7.5, iron, manganese and zinc get harder for plants to take up. Compaction is faster than anyone expects — Colorado State University Extension reports that moist soil can reach 75% of its maximum compaction the first time it is stepped on, and 90% by the fourth pass. And where one soil layer meets a differently textured one, water movement pauses at the boundary rather than passing straight through, so a thin bed of bought topsoil over compacted fill holds water at the seam instead of draining. A UK government case study documents exactly this failure: turf on 150–200 mm of topsoil over un-ripped compacted subsoil, healthy through the first year, failing after.

Every test that reads life needs a pair: the same test, the same day, the same depth, in the ground you are asking about and in ground you already trust. A single unpaired result is a photograph, not a measurement.

The build order

A kingdom of life stands on the layer below it

Nothing above can be bought past a floor that is not there — which is exactly what a lawn over rubble is. Findings and sources at GET /api/ground/layers. The plant, insect and animal specifics assume a temperate maritime climate with dry summers; the working assumption is eastern England, where Cambridge University Botanic Garden records a thirty-year average of 557 mm of rain a year. That is stated, not hidden. The soil physics and all six tests are not climate-specific.

01

Structure and the microbiome

Carbon, moisture, undisturbed structure, and living roots year-round — plants push somewhere in the range of 5–21% of what they photosynthesise into the soil as root exudates, which is how the microbiome gets paid. Compost is the slow, real lever: one application was still measurably raising soil carbon and aggregate stability more than two decades later. Bought mycorrhizal inoculants are not: in a greenhouse test of 25 commercial products from Australia and Europe, over 80% failed to produce measurable root colonisation.

02

Decompaction, mostly by patience

Wood chip belongs on top and never dug in. On heavily compacted clay loam, a five-year UK trial found woodchip mulch alone did nearly as much for bulk density as decompaction machinery. Deep-rooting plants help too — radish roots can go beyond three feet in sixty days. And if vinegar makes your soil fizz, there is free lime in it, and you will not usefully acidify it with sulphur: on soil at 4.5% carbonate, one laboratory saw little to no pH change for three years at any rate. Plant for the pH you have.

03

Plants

On alkaline, sharply drained, low-fertility ground the winning move is not to correct the soil but to plant what already wants it. For invertebrates the lever that works regardless of origin is planting density, with a bias toward native and near-native species — a garden packed with plants beats a sparse one of the "right" ones.

04

Insects

Roughly 70% of solitary bee species nest in underground burrows, so bare, undisturbed ground is habitat rather than neglect. Cutting less helps: a 2025 synthesis in Ecology Letters pooled 28 datasets from 12 European countries. Deadwood carries its own fauna — around 2,000 UK invertebrate species are saproxylic.

05

Animals, honestly

Ponds are disproportionate for their size — two-thirds of Britain's freshwater species are found in them — though the popular claim that a pond is the highest-impact garden intervention circulates without a citation, and this room will not repeat it as fact. A pond without rough ground around it is half a habitat. Feeding birds cuts both ways: a BTO/ZSL analysis found the two most-fed British finches declined fastest where they were fed, because feeders concentrate disease. In the most intensive urban camera-trap study available, supplementary food was the only significant predictor of whether hedgehogs used a garden — not ponds, not log piles, not wild corners.

06

Weather, both ends of it

Drought and flood now arrive in the same year, and a rain garden answers both. The RHS gives numbers to build one by: size it at 20% of the roof area feeding it, allow around 15 cm of depth on soil draining at 5 cm an hour, keep it 5 m from the house, and expect the water gone within 48 hours.
